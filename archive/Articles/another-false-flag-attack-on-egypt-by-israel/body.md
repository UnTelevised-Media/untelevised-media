## The Strike on Damietta

The attack hit the Port of Damietta, a liquefied natural gas hub in Egypt's northern Nile Delta, not far from the Suez Canal — the most critical shipping chokepoint in the world. The Energos Winter, a floating storage and regasification unit, was damaged along with the GasLog Salem, an LNG tanker. Egyptian Cabinet officials confirmed that preliminary investigations determined the fire was caused by a drone strike, though Egyptian authorities stopped short of attributing the attack to any specific actor.

This is not a minor incident. Damietta sits at the mouth of a waterway that handles roughly 12% of global trade. With the Red Sea already paralyzed by Houthi attacks and the Strait of Hormuz in crisis since the U.S.-Iran war erupted in June, the Suez Canal is the last functional route for energy shipments between the Middle East and Europe. A successful attack on a major energy facility at Damietta would send shockwaves through global energy markets and provide a pretext for expanding the conflict into Egyptian territory.

## The Araghchi Statement

Iranian Foreign Minister Seyed Abbas Araghchi responded to the attack on X with characteristic bluntness. *"Egypt is an important friend and partner in the region," *he wrote, *"and its security is of utmost importance to us."* He then directly accused Israel: *"We must all be vigilant against Israeli plots and false-flag operations designed to undermine regional peace."*

The statement is significant. Iran and Egypt have been working to rebuild diplomatic relations after years of estrangement. Cairo has positioned itself as a mediator in the regional conflicts, and Tehran has no strategic interest in alienating Egypt — particularly not by attacking Egyptian infrastructure. Araghchi's framing — that this was an Israeli operation, not an Iranian one — is consistent with Iran's geopolitical calculus: why would Tehran sabotage its own diplomatic overtures to Cairo?

## The Caspian Sea Connection

The context matters. Two days before the Damietta attack, on Saturday July 25, Ukrainian drones struck an Iranian-flagged cargo vessel in the Caspian Sea, killing at least one Iranian sailor. Iran said the ship was carrying civilian cargo; Ukraine said it was transporting military equipment destined for Russia. Iran threatened retaliation, and Iranian state television explicitly named Damietta as a possible target for reprisals against *"Ukrainian interests."*

That detail — Iranian state TV naming Damietta two days before the attack — is the element that Western media immediately seized on to link the drone strike to Iran. And on its surface, the timeline is damning: Iran names the target, Iran's ships get hit in the Caspian, then Damietta gets hit. Case closed, right?

Not so fast. As Araghchi himself pointed out, that very public naming of Damietta is precisely what makes the Iranian attribution suspicious. If Iran were going to conduct a covert drone strike on Egyptian soil, would its state television announce the target in advance? Or does that announcement look more like someone laying the groundwork for a false narrative — creating the *"Iran did it" *story before the strike even happened?

## The Unknown Perpetrator

The truth is, we don't know with certainty who launched the drone that hit Damietta. What we know is this:

The attack damaged two vessels at one of the most sensitive energy hubs on the Mediterranean. No one claimed it. Iran had publicly named Damietta as a target two days earlier — a detail that could be evidence of Iranian planning, or evidence of someone else laying the groundwork for a convenient attribution. Iran's top diplomat immediately called it an Israeli false flag — and his reasoning is sound: Iran has nothing to gain from attacking Egypt and everything to gain from maintaining its diplomatic offensive with Cairo. Israel has everything to gain from expanding the war.

The drone that hit Damietta may have been Iranian. But the pattern — the unclaimed strike, the pre-positioned attribution, the target that serves Israeli strategic interests far more than Iranian ones — should make every observer skeptical of the easy narrative.
