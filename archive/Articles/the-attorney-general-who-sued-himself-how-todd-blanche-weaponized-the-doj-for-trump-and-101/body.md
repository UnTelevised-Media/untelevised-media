## The $1.8 Billion Slush Fund

In January 2026, Donald Trump filed a $10 billion lawsuit against the Internal Revenue Service, demanding compensation because a former IRS contractor had leaked his tax returns to the New York Times during his first term. The lawsuit was filed in the Southern District of Florida — a jurisdiction with a reputation for being favorable to Trump — and seemingly outside the statute of limitations, which Judge Williams noted in her ruling.

What made the lawsuit extraordinary was that Trump was, in effect, suing his own administration. He controlled both sides of the litigation: the plaintiff (himself and his sons) and the defendant (the IRS, an agency under his executive control). The DOJ's job was to zealously defend the United States against the suit. Instead, Todd Blanche — Trump's former personal defense attorney, who had represented him in criminal cases including the January 6 indictment — did not contest a single claim.

By May, Blanche announced a "settlement." Trump would drop the $10 billion suit. In exchange, the federal government would establish a $1.776 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund" to compensate anyone who alleged the Biden administration had "weaponized" the legal system against them — a category that primarily covers January 6 rioters convicted of assaulting law enforcement officers and destroying federal property. The money would come from the Treasury, i.e., American taxpayers.

The deal also included a separate DOJ order — signed solely by Blanche, with no IRS signature — that "FOREVER BARRED" the government from auditing Trump, his sons Donald Jr. and Eric, and the Trump Organization over past tax returns. No one from the IRS signed the tax exemption order. Only Blanche did. The IRS reports to the Treasury Department, not the Attorney General.

Sen. Ron Wyden (D-Ore.) called the IRS deal "an obscene act of corrupt self-dealing." He was right. The New York Times reported in 2020 that Trump potentially owed as much as $100 million from an adverse ruling in a decade-long audit. Blanche's order extinguished that liability with the stroke of a pen.

## The Judge's Reckoning

On July 14, U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams, an Obama appointee, released a blistering order that laid bare the entire scheme.

"There was never adverseness between the Parties; there was never a case or controversy; and there was never a question as to who would prevail," Williams wrote, finding that Trump controlled the defendant because of his own executive order defining his authority over all federal agencies.

"The nature of the suit itself and the conduct of the Parties and counsel from its filing make plain that this was an attempt to use the Court to provide some legitimacy to an agreement to confer immunity to people and entities affiliated with the President and to earmark billions of dollars from American taxpayers to redress grievances not defined in the law."

Williams found that the DOJ had "abandoned its responsibility to zealously defend the interests of the United States" and adopted "the strategy of creating a 'slush fund disguised as a settlement, and then doling the money out to whatever constituency the Executive wants bankrolled.'"

She ordered monetary sanctions against Trump, demanded that the opinion be sent to the New York State Bar and the D.C. Bar for disciplinary proceedings, and barred Trump from describing the deal as a legitimate settlement in any future official proceedings.

After facing bipartisan backlash and the court injunction, Blanche publicly claimed the DOJ would "not be moving forward" with the slush fund portion of the settlement. But days later, in written answers to the Senate Judiciary Committee, Blanche refused to rule out reviving the fund in the future, declined to answer whether he had pressured IRS officials to sign the settlement, and refused to say whether it was "appropriate" to grant Trump and his family criminal immunity from tax audits. The audit exemption, HuffPost confirmed, remains in effect.

## 101 Judges vs. the Attorney General

On June 22, 2026, more than 100 current and former federal and state judges — joined by two legal advocacy groups, Democracy Defenders Fund and Lawyers Defending American Democracy — filed a 73-page ethics complaint against Blanche with the New York State Attorney Grievance Committee.

The complaint documented roughly a dozen instances of alleged misconduct, including:

- **Conflict of interest:** Blanche continued to represent Trump's interests while overseeing DOJ investigations — most critically the Epstein Files investigation, where Trump is referenced more than 5,300 times, and the questioning of Ghislaine Maxwell, whom Blanche oversaw before her sudden transfer to a minimum-security prison, contradicting Bureau of Prisons policy for sex offenders.
- **Throwing the IRS case:** The judges alleged Blanche provided legal advice to Trump or threw the IRS case in the president's favor while supposedly representing the United States against the president.
- **Violating professional ethics:** Blanche violated the New York Rules of Professional Conduct through his conduct in the settlement and his representation of a former client while serving as the nation's chief law enforcement officer.

Scott Harshbarger, the co-founder of Lawyers Defending American Democracy and a two-term Massachusetts attorney general, said: "The damage over these many months to the once esteemed Department of Justice has been incalculable."

The DOJ's response was characteristically dismissive. A spokesperson called the judges' complaint a "pathetic stunt conjured up by activist judges" who are "desperate" for attention from the liberal media.

On July 22, the New York Attorney Grievance Committee — which has disciplinary authority over Blanche's law license — declined to open its own investigation and instead referred the complaint to the DOJ's Office of Professional Responsibility, effectively asking Blanche's own department to investigate him. On July 25, the judges appealed.

## 1,200 Former DOJ Employees Say No

The opposition to Blanche's nomination extends far beyond the judiciary. On July 7, more than 1,200 former Justice Department employees from administrations spanning both parties — with thousands of years of collective experience — sent a letter to the Senate Judiciary Committee urging rejection of Blanche's confirmation as Attorney General.

The letter, organized by Justice Connection, accused Blanche of degrading the DOJ's apolitical workforce by "demonizing career attorneys" and creating a "culture of fear" within the department. It described his tenure as an assault on the institutional norms that have historically insulated the DOJ from political interference.

## The Confirmation Charade

Senate confirmation hearings were held on July 15 and 16. Even Republican senators expressed concern. Sen. "He said 'No, it wasn't prospective,'" Durbin told reporters. "That was his defense. Having said that, that's not much of a defense, as far as I'm concerned. Why would they be exempt from tax laws because of some question about whether somebody disclosed his income tax return?"

The New York City Bar Association also formally opposed Blanche's nomination, an extraordinary step from one of the nation's most prestigious bar organizations.

When the Attorney Grievance Committee asks the DOJ to investigate itself, when the Senate confirms a man 101 judges have condemned, when the rule of law is reduced to a punchline, the problem isn't one corrupt lawyer.
