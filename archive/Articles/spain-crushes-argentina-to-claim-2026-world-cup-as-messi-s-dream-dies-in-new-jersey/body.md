EAST RUTHERFORD, N.J. — Spain did not just beat Argentina on Sunday. They strangled them. For 106 minutes, La Roja held Lionel Messi and the defending champions in a vice grip so suffocating that the greatest player of his generation barely touched the ball, and when he finally did, there was nothing left to give.

Ferran Torres broke the deadlock in the 106th minute of extra time, thumping home an 8-yard finish after Nico Williams outmuscled Nahuel Molina at the back post, heading the ball back across a scrambling Emiliano Martínez. The goal didn't just win Spain a second World Cup — it buried Argentina's bid to become the first repeat champion since Brazil in 1962, and it likely ended Messi's World Cup career in the cruelest possible fashion.

The final score: Spain 1, Argentina 0. The real scoreline: Spain 20 shots, 12 on target, 31 touches in the penalty area. Argentina: two shots total, zero on target for 117 minutes, eight touches in the box. The expected goals told the full story — 1.94 for Spain, 0.20 for Argentina. This wasn't a final. It was an execution with a 90-minute delay.

## A Tournament of Two Halves

The 2026 World Cup had been Messi's stage. The 39-year-old had dragged Argentina through the knockout rounds on sheer will and late-game wizardry — 10 of Argentina's 19 tournament goals came in the 80th minute or later, including seven in the 90th minute or beyond, both World Cup records. Against Egypt in the Round of 16, he conjured two goals to flip the deficit. Against Switzerland in the quarterfinals, Argentina needed extra time again. In the semifinal against England, two late strikes once more.

Spain took the opposite path. Luis de la Fuente's side never trailed the entire tournament. They breezed past Austria in the Round of 32, edged Portugal and Belgium with late Mikel Merino goals, then demolished France 2-0 in the semifinal behind goals from Mikel Oyarzabal and Pedro Porro. They allowed just one goal all tournament — a defensive record that underscored the system's dominance.

That system was on full display from the opening whistle on Sunday. Lamine Yamal, who turned 19 last week, found dangerous positions early. Spain's pressing forced Argentina into turnovers before they could breathe. Teenage center-back Pau Cubarsí — who would go on to win Young Player of the Tournament — marshalled a backline that gave Messi nothing.

By halftime, the numbers were already damning. Argentina had zero shots, zero corner kicks, zero touches in the penalty area, and just 35 percent possession. They had scored 19 goals in seven matches to reach this final. In the first 45 minutes of it, they managed to complete exactly zero attacking sequences of note.

## The Red Card That sealed It

The match lurched into stoppage time still scoreless, and that's when Argentina's discipline finally collapsed. Midfielder Enzo Fernández, already carrying a yellow card, launched into a late challenge on Spain's Pau Cubarsí in the 90+3 minute, sending the young defender twisting into the air before crashing to the turf. Referee Slavko Vinčić reached for a second yellow. Argentina was down to 10 men with 30 minutes of extra time still to play.

Martínez, Argentina's goalkeeper, had already broken the World Cup final record with 11 saves — repeatedly denying Yamal, Williams, and Torres to keep his team's flickering hopes alive. Moments after the red card, Yamal stood over a free kick 25 yards out and curled a gorgeous effort over the wall. Martínez read it, pushed it aside with both hands. But the reprieve was borrowed time.

In the 93rd minute of extra time, Martínez pulled off another stunning save, flashing to his right to tip Williams' glancing header from close range. Williams then thought he'd scored from the resulting scramble, but Mikel Merino's foul nullified the goal. Argentina was drowning and somehow still treading water.

Then came the moment. Pedro Porro lifted the ball to the back post in the 106th minute. Williams beat Molina to the header. Martínez had committed, leaving his net exposed. Williams nodded it back across goal. Ferran Torres arrived on cue and buried it.

## Messi's Final Act

The Athletic called Argentina's performance "the worst from any team in a major final in living memory." That's not hyperbole. In a sport where the final is supposed to be the pinnacle, Argentina produced nothing — no shots, no patterns, no fight until it was far too late. They registered their first attempt of any kind in the 117th minute.

Messi, who had been "better than ever" at this tournament according to multiple reports, was rendered invisible by Spain's suffocating structure. Without the ball, without space, without a teammate capable of holding possession under pressure, the 39-year-old had nowhere to go. After the final whistle, he sat on the MetLife Stadium turf, staring at nothing, as Spanish players celebrated around him. He was red-faced and in tears during the medal ceremony. Lionel Scaloni broke up shoving matches between players from both sides.

It was a fittingly grim end to what had been a remarkable individual tournament for Messi — he took the Silver Ball behind Spain's Rodri, who won the Golden Ball as the tournament's best player. But the team around him had nothing left. The late-game magic that had carried Argentina through the knockout rounds never arrived. Against a Spanish side that simply refused to break, there was no miracle to be found.

## Spain's Quiet Dynasty

Spain's victory extends their unbeaten run to 38 matches over more than two years under de la Fuente, a former youth coach who has now collected the European Championship, the Nations League, and the World Cup. They are the first country to simultaneously hold both the men's and women's World Cup titles. Unai Simón, who barely faced a shot all tournament, won the Golden Glove. Rodri, the metronome at the base of midfield who returned from a significant knee injury to dominate the tournament, took the Golden Ball.

Kylian Mbappé, who wasn't present for the ceremony, won his second consecutive Golden Boot with 10 goals — and became the World Cup's all-time leading scorer in the process. But it was Spain's day. From the opening whistle to the final, they never stopped. They pressed, they probed, they suffocated. They made the defending champions look like they didn't belong on the same pitch.

In the end, the 2026 World Cup didn't produce a classic final. It produced something more definitive: a statement that this Spanish generation — built without a single Real Madrid starter, constructed around system and discipline rather than individual brilliance — is the best team in the world. The trophy lift, with Rodri on the podium and Trump and Infantino lingering nearby, cemented what the 90-plus minutes of football had already made clear.

Spain didn't just win the World Cup. They took it.
